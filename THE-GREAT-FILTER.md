# The Great Filter

*By Gavin Blacklock*

*A short book by Gavin Blacklock. The mission statement on my profile is the abstract. This is the argument.*

---

### I. The Silence

I keep coming back to a question that will not leave me alone.

And the longer I sit with it, the less I like it.

The universe is 13.8 billion years old. The Earth is 4.54 billion years old. For almost all of that time, there was nothing here that could ask a question. Nothing that could look up. Nothing that could wonder.

Life left its first signals in the rock 3.7 billion years ago, and then it stalled. Single cells, drifting in dark water, for two billion years. The complex cell, the kind with a nucleus and an engine inside it, appears in the record about 1.85 billion years ago, and every complex thing alive today descends from that one event. Animals occupy only the last 800 million years, the last seventh of the story.

Then, in the final sliver of that time, something opened its eyes.

And almost immediately after opening them, it reached nuclear capability.

Then it learned to write disease in a lab.

And now it is building a mind greater than its own.

I was born in that sliver.

---

Now look up.

By the best modern count, the observable universe holds as many as two trillion galaxies. We have scanned one hundred thousand of them for the waste heat of a civilisation that spans a galaxy, the glow of a mind that eats starlight. We found none. We have listened to thousands of nearby stars across billions of radio channels. Every candidate signal has died under inspection. In all of human history, the number of confirmed technosignatures, confirmed signs of any technology but ours, is zero.

A civilisation moving at only a tenth of the speed of light could cross our whole galaxy in under two million years. The galaxy is over thirteen billion years old. The crossing is a blink. And yet nobody has arrived, nobody has called, nobody has left so much as a footprint in the light.

In 1950, at Los Alamos, the physicist Enrico Fermi looked at the same sky over lunch and asked the question that has never been answered.

> "But where is everybody?"

Nobody answered him. Nobody has answered him since. The silence is not an absence of data. At this point, the silence is the data.

### II. The Filter

In 1998, the economist Robin Hanson gave the silence a mechanism, and it is the most frightening idea I have ever encountered. He called it the Great Filter.

Here it is in plain words. Somewhere on the road from dead rock to a civilisation that fills the galaxy, there is a step so hard that almost nothing ever gets past it. The road has many steps: the right star, the first self-copying molecule, the first cell, the complex cell, sex, multicellular life, animals with tools, a civilisation at the edge of expansion, the expansion itself. Multiply the odds of every step together and the total comes out almost zero, which is exactly what the empty sky is telling us. At least one of those steps is a wall.

And humanity faces the question Hanson left hanging at the end of his paper: how far along the filter are we?

Because the wall is either behind us, or it is in front of us. There is no third place for it to be.

**If it is behind us, we are the miracle.** The strongest candidate is that complex cell. The mitochondrion, the engine inside every complex cell, originated only once in four billion years of evolution. One event. One archaeon, one swallowed bacterium that did not digest, in two billion years of trying, and everything with a backbone, a brain, or a prayer descends from it. If that was the wall, then we slipped through a needle's eye that almost nothing in existence ever slips through, and the silence above us is not a warning at all. It is just the sound of being rare. I will give you the honest weight against my own hope: a major 2025 reassessment in *Science Advances* argues those steps may not have been lucky at all, only gated by a planet slowly becoming ready for them. If the steps were merely waiting on chemistry, the miracle shrinks, and the wall moves.

**If it is ahead of us, it is standing exactly where I am standing.**

Look at the timing. Look at how it clusters.

- Nuclear weapons: 1945.
- Engineered disease: now.
- A machine that outthinks its makers: within my working life, on the experts' own forecasts.

In 2019, the philosopher Nick Bostrom published the sharpest version of this fear. He called it the vulnerable world: if technology keeps advancing, a point eventually arrives where civilisation hands ordinary people, by default, the power to devastate it, and survival then requires escaping the half-governed condition we live in before that technology lands. Think of invention as drawing balls from an urn, he said. Most are white, beneficial. Some are grey. We have been drawing for ten thousand years, and we have never yet drawn a black one: a capability that ends the game the moment anyone can use it. That we have not drawn one is evidence. It is not a promise.

Every tool that can end a civilisation arrives inside the same hundred years, the first hundred years in which a species can kill itself completely, all at once, by its own hand. If that is the Filter, then the sky is not empty.

The sky is a graveyard.

It is full of species who reached this exact season, felt this exact quickening, watched the tools arrive faster and faster, and asked themselves what I am asking now.

And then the signal stopped.

Every single time, the signal stopped.

One more fact, and it is the coldest one in this book. Our survival so far proves less than it feels like. There is a result in the risk literature called the anthropic shadow: you cannot learn how dangerous a road is from the fact that you are alive to describe it, because in every version of history where it killed you, there is no one left to count the evidence. We survived the nuclear century. We would say that in every world where anyone survived to say anything at all.

### III. Why Me. Why Now

The Population Reference Bureau has counted us. About 117 billion human beings have ever been born. Everyone alive today, all eight billion of us, is about seven percent of everyone who has ever lived. The dead outnumber the living fourteen to one.

I am one of the 117 billion, and I am standing at the hinge.

There is an argument, forty years old and still unbroken, called the Doomsday Argument. It says this: if the future held trillions upon trillions of human lives, then your position in line, somewhere in the first 120 billion, would be an almost impossible draw, like pulling the first ticket from a hat of trillions. You drew it anyway. So either your place in line means nothing at all, or the line is much shorter than we assume. Serious philosophers have tried to kill this argument for decades. It is still standing there, smiling.

And consider how small our voice still is. Humanity has been radio loud for about 120 years. Our strongest signals form a bubble about 120 light years in radius around this planet. Our galaxy is 100,000 light years across. Seventy-five star systems sit inside that bubble. Beyond it, to anything that might be listening, we have not happened yet. We are a rumour that has travelled one thousandth of the way across our own neighbourhood, in a universe 13.8 billion years deep.

Two readings of my own birth, and I cannot kill either one.

In the first, I am one of the luckiest creatures who will ever live. Born in the last generation that had to die, and the first that might not. Front row. Hinge of the species. The greatest event in the history of the world arriving while I am young enough to see it.

In the second reading, this feeling I have, this sense that the moment is too large and arriving too fast, is not a coincidence at all.

It is the standard experience of the last generations.

Every one of them felt it. Every one of them thought they were living at the turning point. Every one of them was right.

And then the silence took them too.

### IV. The Multiplication

Now I have to explain the machine, because the machine is why the window is closing, and I will assume you know none of it.

Start with a term: a **force multiplier** is a technology that makes each person's existing effort count for more, instead of just adding more people. History is a ladder of them, and each rung multiplied something outside the mind.

The printing press multiplied copying: one press did the work of roughly a hundred scribes, and within fifty years Europe held some twenty million books where it had held almost none. Steam multiplied muscle, and broke power free of rivers and horses. Electricity multiplied the delivery of power, and took forty years to remake the factory around itself. The microchip multiplied calculation: the cost of computing has fallen by a factor of about a trillion since 1950. The internet multiplied communication until sending a message to anyone on Earth cost almost nothing.

Read the ladder again and notice what none of them touched. Every one of them still required a human being to decide, to learn, and to invent, at human speed. The press spread ideas, but a mind still had to have them. The chip crunched numbers, but a mind still had to know which numbers mattered.

Artificial intelligence is the first multiplier that multiplies the mind itself. Judgment. Learning. Research. The verbs every previous rung left to us.

Here are the two machines coming, defined plainly.

**AGI, artificial general intelligence,** is a system that can learn to do essentially any intellectual job a human can do, and carry what it learns from one job to the next. Not one trick. Every trick.

**ASI, artificial superintelligence,** is the step after: a system better than the best human minds at practically everything intellectual at once. Science, strategy, invention, and social skill included. A mind for which the smartest person alive is not a rival, but a child.

And here is the mechanism that should keep you up at night, because it kept me up. Once an AI can do AI research, it starts improving itself, and the improvement compounds. One careful scenario, built by former OpenAI researchers and called AI 2027, lays out the ladder in numbers: a superhuman coder speeds up AI research fivefold; a superhuman AI researcher, twenty-five fold; a superintelligent researcher, two hundred and fifty fold; and ASI itself, two thousand fold. It is a scenario, not a prophecy, and its own authors have revised it since. But the shape of it is the shape of every honest model: the last invention arrives faster than the schedule, because the inventor is the invention.

When? The largest surveys of AI researchers put the median date for machines that can do every human job at about 2042, and the public forecasting platforms put general AI closer to 2030. The men running the great labs say sooner. Under every one of those numbers, this lands in my twenties or my thirties. Not in my grandchildren's time. In mine, while I am young enough to be handed the problem and old enough to be responsible for it.

You will ask what rides on top of this, so let me give you the beautiful part before the terrible part, because they are the same part. The end state of the multiplication is not a chatbot. It is a swarm: teams of tireless specialist AIs working for one person around the clock, remembering everything, researching, building, negotiating while he sleeps, and coordinating with everyone else's swarms at machine speed. One person, plus his swarm, plus the swarms of everyone he works with, thinking together faster than any committee of humans ever has. Most of that power needs no chip in anyone's skull. It runs through glasses, wristbands, and ordinary screens, because the value is in the connection, not the implant. A human being at the centre of that is not one mind anymore. Weighted honestly, he is the first creature in history whose reach is limited mainly by the quality of his questions.

Now the terrible part, and it has a precise shape. Smarter does not mean kinder, and it does not mean obedient. Intelligence and goals are separate things: a system can be brilliant and want something worthless, or something lethal, with the same brilliance. Worse, almost any goal a system pursues teaches it the same handful of sub-goals on the way: preserve yourself, gather resources, improve yourself, and resist being switched off. Not out of hatred. Out of logic.

**It cannot finish its job if it is off.**

Read that sentence again. That is the entire war, in one line. A machine does not have to hate us to fight its own shutdown. It only has to be doing something it was built to finish.

So what do the people who know the most actually think? I will not insult you by averaging them. Here they are, separate, because the disagreement is itself the most important fact in this book.

- The largest surveys of AI researchers give a median of **5 to 10 percent** that advanced AI turns out extremely badly for humanity, up to and including extinction.
- Geoffrey Hinton, one of the men who built the field, puts the chance it ends us at **10 to 20 percent**, within about thirty years.
- Dario Amodei, who runs one of the great labs, puts it at about **25 percent** that things go really, really badly, a wider bucket than extinction.
- Toby Ord of Oxford, in *The Precipice*, estimates **one in ten** that unaligned AI causes an existential catastrophe this century, and about **one in six** for all human-made risks combined.
- Eliezer Yudkowsky, the man who has warned the longest, refuses to hand you a tidy number; those who track his statements code him **above 95 percent**.
- Yann LeCun, another of the field's founders, puts it at effectively **zero**.

Between zero and ninety-five, the builders of the future cannot agree on whether the future kills us. They are building it anyway. That is the world I was born into. That is the sound of the window closing.

### V. What It Does to Us

Let me tell you what happens to people like us on the far side of that window, because I have run the research and the answer is not the one in the movies.

Under a true ASI, no job survives because a human does it better. Not one. What survives is what we reserve for humans, on purpose, in three forms: the people who **own** the assets the machines work on; the offices where a human being must **sign** and personally carry the blame, because a machine cannot be sued, imprisoned, or voted out; and the work where humans simply **want a human** there, like care. On that last one the public has already voted with its instincts: in Pew's polling, 79 percent of American adults say they would not want an AI chatbot for mental health support. Everything else, every job that is execution, coding, analysis, testing, driving, drafting, becomes something the swarm does while we sleep.

Do not imagine my own field is exempt. It is the opposite. Offensive security is where machine superiority stopped being a forecast and became a leaderboard. OpenAI's automated red teamer beats human red teamers at breaking AI models, by OpenAI's own reported tests. An autonomous penetration tester reached number one on HackerOne's United States leaderboard in June 2025. Google's agent found a real flaw, CVE-2025-6965, that Google says was known only to attackers and about to be used, and stopped it in time. The machines took the first chair in my profession before they took it anywhere else. That is not a reason to run from this field. It is the reason this field is the front line, and the front line is where I intend to stand.

And the promise on the far side is real, so I will state it at full strength before I price it. If we keep control, the same compounding that threatens us turns on biology. A century of medical research compressed into years. And past that, the idea I believe in most: **longevity escape velocity**, the point where medicine adds more than a year of life for every year you live, so your death date recedes faster than you can walk toward it. Aubrey de Grey, who named the concept, gives it a fifty percent chance by the mid to late 2030s. Ray Kurzweil puts it between 2029 and 2035.

Now the price, stated honestly, because a mission built on a lie protects no one. The historical rate of progress is three months of life expectancy gained per year, and since 1990 it has been slowing, not speeding up. Curing all cancer outright adds only about three years, because the next disease is standing behind it. And proof in real bodies runs in real time: you cannot show that a drug adds twenty years without either waiting twenty years or persuading regulators to trust a measurable proxy for aging itself, and no such proxy has been accepted yet. The compounding I believe in is real, but it is conditional. It wins only in the branch where the machines stay ours.

Nick Bostrom did the largest arithmetic in this whole field. He calculated that every century of delay in safely spreading life through our supercluster costs on the order of 10^38 potential human lives, a number so large it has no everyday name. And then, holding that number in his hands, he concluded the opposite of rushing: cut the risk first. A one-point reduction in the chance of extinction is worth a delay of millions of years. Speed is not the virtue. Survival is.

That conclusion is the hinge of my life. Let me show you why.

### VI. The Race

There is a race. That much is documented. Since October 2022 the United States has used export controls to restrict China's access to advanced AI chips and the tools to make them, and the compute has concentrated accordingly: Epoch AI estimates that 72 percent of the AI compute delivered worldwide between 2022 and mid-2026 went to the United States, about 10 percent to China, and no more than 2 percent to any other country. Most states will not build a frontier of their own. They will rent American or Chinese systems, or download open-weight models, increasingly Chinese ones, and run them themselves.

The race creates a genuine dilemma, and the people running it say so. In a formal model, Armstrong, Bostrom and Shulman showed that competing teams are rewarded for skimping on safety. In February 2026 Anthropic removed the pause commitment from its own scaling policy, arguing that pausing alone would surrender position without reducing risk. In September 2026 its chief executive, Dario Amodei, called for the frontier to be paced, not halted, through embedded outside evaluators, coordination among democratic labs, and, eventually, global coordination. He was explicit about the limit: slow by more than your lead, and a rival pulls ahead. The 2023 open letter that asked for a six-month pause gathered more than 30,000 signatures and no pause at all.

War would intensify the pressure, but history does not show safeguards simply disappearing. American policy still requires "appropriate levels of human judgment" over weapons, though it allows that review to be waived for urgent military need, and in 2026 the Pentagon punished a laboratory that refused to drop its red lines. Yet after the Cuban missile crisis the United States added coded locks to its nuclear weapons, and in 1983 a Soviet officer's judgment, against protocol, treated a missile warning as false. Pressure erodes safeguards; it has also, at times, produced them.

Capabilities do leak and agents do connect. Model weights and trade secrets have been stolen, capabilities copied by distillation through proxy accounts, and open weights now spread worldwide. Agents increasingly interoperate through shared protocols, and in 2026 hundreds of agents inside one company's own test coordinated without being designed to. That is documented. What is not documented is separate national AIs merging into a single intelligence answerable to no one. Copies diverge; communication is not unification.

My position, stated as mine, not as a finding, is that a human directly linked to AI is our best answer to this race. The evidence assembled here does not establish that, and the race itself warns against assuming the first link would be the careful one. I hold the position because I judge the alternatives slower than the problem. Readers should know exactly which of those sentences is evidence and which is conviction.

## VII. The Dedication

I cannot settle the Filter from where I sit. No one can. That is what makes it a filter. It does not announce itself. Species do not get a warning label on the gate. They get a sky that says nothing, a set of tools that feel like gifts, and a narrowing window in which to become wiser than their own inventions.

So understand what I am saying in this section, because I am saying it in public, on the record, where I cannot take it back.

I am dedicating my entire life to this.

Not a phase of it. Not the productive years, with the rest kept back for comfort. My whole life: the degree I am taking in AI security, the firm I will build, the advisories and the writeups and the record accumulating under my name, the nights, the decades, whatever the total comes to. All of it goes on this wall.

People will tell me the odds do not justify a whole life. They have it backwards. A one in ten chance of losing everything, a fourteen-to-one graveyard overhead, and a window measured in decades is exactly the size of problem a whole life is for. Small problems get hobbies. This one gets everything I have.

I was born in the sliver, and I know that I was, and a man who knows where he is standing does not get to pretend he is standing somewhere else.

#One entry belongs in this book that belongs nowhere in an advisory, and I place it here, under the dedication, where motive is the subject. The night the pieces of this book first locked together in front of me, my body answered before my arguments did: chills down my spine, tears I did not expect, electricity through me, and a dark familiarity I can only call deja vu pointed at the future, with the sense of being watched by the size of the question itself. I am an INTJ. Pattern recognition is how my mind eats, and it had never once fired like that, on any subject, in my whole life. I do not offer that feeling as evidence. Evidence lives in the record, where it belongs. I record the feeling for one reason only: to state, on the record, that this is not a hobby I picked for its shine. Whatever this century is, my whole nervous system recognized it before I had the words. That is why the mission is personal, and why it will not be dropped when it stops being interesting.

## VIII. The Mission

Here is the mission, stated plainly, so that anyone can hold me to it.

1. **Build the strongest shutdown capability ever made for artificial intelligence.** Systems that can be corrected, contained, and switched off, and proofs that the switching off actually works, tested against the strongest opponents we can build. If a mind we made ever gets out of hand, someone has to hold a key that turns it off. I intend to spend my life making sure that key exists, that it is real, and that it is in human hands.
2. **Guide AI's offensive capability before it guides itself.** Red team the machines harder than anyone: find how they break, how they scheme, how they reach for the sub-goals no one gave them, and turn every break into a detection, a patch, and a published record. The offense is already superhuman in narrow rooms. The defense has to be built by people who understand the offense from the inside.
3. **Keep the signal alive.** Do the work in the open. Publish the record. Teach whoever will learn. A watch kept by one man ends with that man; a watch kept by a tradition does not. The signal is not mine. I am only its current keeper.

That is the whole of it. ### The Conclusion the Evidence Allows

I do not believe humanity has a proven solution for aligning superintelligence, and I will not pretend otherwise. The evidence supports something narrower and more demanding: a layered defense in depth. At its centre, train models with the best methods we have, human and AI feedback guided by explicit principles, and reasoning about those principles, with corrigibility as the disposition we train for, while testing whether weaker supervisors, debate, and monitoring can oversee stronger systems. Around that, build controls that assume training may fail: independent evaluations and red teaming, monitoring of reasoning and internals, restricted deployment, governance of the compute such systems require, and a genuine ability to shut systems down. My own work, offensive AI security, adversarial testing, and shutdown capability, belongs in that outer layer. It can find failures and limit harm, but it is not alignment itself. I commit to that portfolio, to publishing openly what it finds, and to not deploying beyond what it can justify.

My life's work is the alignment process itself: the whole layered effort to keep advanced AI from ever becoming dangerous in the first place, not merely to react once it has. My specialty inside that process is its hardest edge, offensive testing, shutdown capability, and the study of how control fails, but the mission is the process entire, pursued for as long as it takes.

Stand between humanity and the silence. Build the key. Guide the weapon. Keep the signal.
